# UK CCI Regulation: Complete Study Guide

Source: FCA Policy Statement PS25/20 (December 2025), the CCI Regulations 2024 (SI 2024/1198), and the FCA instrument 2025/52. This guide explains every step, component and rule, with no calculation simulation.

## 1. What a CCI is and why it exists

CCI stands for Consumer Composite Investment. It is the UK replacement for the PRIIPs KID and the UCITS KIID. A CCI is an investment whose returns depend on the performance of, or changes in the value of, underlying or reference assets.

**Products in scope:** open-ended funds, closed-ended funds (CEIFs), structured products and structured deposits, contracts for difference (CFDs), insurance-based investment products (IBIPs), other complex products like derivatives, and recognised funds.

**Excluded:** vanilla corporate bonds (plain vanilla listed bonds), bonds tracking EURIBOR and similar interbank rates, pension products, pure protection insurance, and units in authorised contractual schemes (ACS) and qualified investor schemes (QIS).

**Non-retail opt-out:** a CCI falls outside the Product Disclosure sourcebook (DISC) if its communications are clearly marked as not for retail AND the manufacturer takes steps to ensure it is not directed at retail investors, including setting an appropriate distribution strategy. The old £50,000 minimum investment criterion was removed. Distributors must not direct or sell any CCI without an up-to-date product summary to a retail client.

**Overseas funds:** an overseas fund can only be marketed to UK retail investors if it is a recognised scheme, through the Overseas Funds Regime (OFR) or section 272 FSMA.

## 2. The timeline

- 8 December 2025: FCA publishes final rules, 18-month implementation period starts.
- 6 April 2026: legislation commences, optional transition opens. Manufacturers may produce a product summary or keep their KID or KIID.
- 8 June 2027: full go-live. Firms in scope must comply with the whole regime.

Only the ability to produce and make available a product summary turns on before 8 June 2027. Distributors' obligations stay as just providing the relevant existing document until then.

## 3. Who does what

### Manufacturers

A manufacturer is a person who creates, develops, designs, issues, manages,&#32;

&#32;or carries out a CCI. The steps are:

1. Create the product.
2. Calculate the core product information using the detailed methodologies in the rules. HZ
3. Prepare a machine-readable file.
4. Prepare a consumer-friendly product summary.
5. Make both available on a publicly accessible website.
6. Share other information with distributors as the rules require.
7. Review and update the information when required, and at least once a year.
8. Respond to feedback from distributors.

The exception: a firm that is the only distributor of products it manufactures does not need to produce a machine-readable file.

Where several firms co-manufacture, they agree their division of responsibilities in writing. For CEIFs, the AIFM will carry the vast majority of the requirements.

### Distributors

A distributor offers, advises on or sells a CCI, or provides investment services relating to one to retail investors.

1. Review the manufacturer's information and product summary.
2. Make the product summary available pre-sale for consumers who would find it useful.
3. Highlight, at a minimum: a brief explanation of the product; the ongoing costs figure and other relevant costs such as one-off costs; the risk and return score with a brief explanation and any required warnings.
4. Decide whether additional explanatory information would help.
5. Provide the product summary, unamended, in a durable medium at the point of sale or shortly after.
6. Share feedback with manufacturers, and report problems or inaccuracies up the chain.

Distributors cannot make their own product summary or change the manufacturer's one. They can use the underlying data to build their own consumer journeys.

## 4. The product summary

There is no prescribed template or page limit. The standard is what works best to inform consumers, underpinned by the Consumer Duty. The minimum content is:

- General product information (name, identifiers, objectives, penalties for early exit, FOS or FSCS cover, how to complain, with an option to link to complaints information).
- Costs and charges.
- Risk and return.
- Past performance.

Communications must be fair, clear and not misleading. No target market description is required from the manufacturer in the summary, and no domicile requirement was added.

## 5. Costs and charges

The headline cost is the Ongoing Charge Figure (OCF), shown both as a percentage and as a pounds-and-pence amount. One-off costs and transaction costs sit outside it. The FCA dropped the single aggregated summary cost indicator because it implied one-off costs were payable every 12 months.

### The cost components, one by one

| Component | Rule | Where it appears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ongoing costs (OCF) | The mandatory headline figure, as a percentage and in pounds and pence | Headline of the cost section |
| One-off costs | Entry and exit charges where they exist, disclosed prominently but separately from ongoing costs | Separate line, not added to the OCF |
| Explicit transaction costs | A numerical disclosure is required, presented separately from ongoing costs. Mark-ups and mark-downs count as explicit | Separate number, flexible placement |
| Implicit transaction costs | No longer required. The bid-ask spread is treated as implicit | Not disclosed |
| Performance fees | Must be made clear to consumers, including in underlying CCIs and CEIFs, with any conflicts of interest | Disclosed for standalone and underlying funds |
| Zero-cost categories | Do not need to appear in the summary, but the manufacturer confirms them in writing to distributors in the machine-readable file | Underlying information file |

### Pull-through of underlying costs

When a CCI invests in other CCIs, the underlying costs are pulled through into a synthetic OCF. The FCA dropped its earlier idea of exempting passive funds, so this applies to active and passive funds alike. Firms may layer the disclosure by also breaking out the underlying funds' costs.

Closed-ended investment funds (CEIFs) are treated differently. Their costs must be transparent and clearly presented, and the weighted average cost to NAV ratio is calculated with the same methodology, but those costs are not added into the investing fund's OCF.

### What the OCF no longer includes

No CCI is required to include gearing costs or the maintenance of real assets in its ongoing cost calculation. The same rule now applies across all CCI categories, so costs reflect the cost of managing the fund and stay comparable.

### Special cases

- **Structured products:** costs the investor will incur are disclosed where they are known in advance. If maturity is under one year, costs are shown over the lifetime of the product, since there is no 12-month history to rely on.
- **Non-sterling products:** the cost figures are still shown in pounds and pence, because the regime is designed for UK consumers and the figures are illustrative.
- **Yield-reduction metric:** the PRIIPs Reduction in Yield methodology is not required under CCIs.

### Link to MiFID disclosures

MiFID costs and charges disclosure serves a different purpose. It covers the total cost of investing, including distribution and advice costs that cannot appear on a product summary. The product costs used there are those required by DISC: ongoing costs, one-off costs and transaction costs, plus actual performance fees where known in ongoing disclosures. No prescriptive method is set for the cumulative effect of costs, and a one-year period is acceptable.

## 6. Risk and return

The score is now called the risk and return score, not just a risk score, to give a balanced message about potential returns as well as risk. It runs on a 1 to 10 scale instead of the PRIIPs and UCITS 1 to 7 scale. The FCA's behavioural testing showed a slight improvement in consumer comprehension with the 10-point scale.

### Step 1: Measure volatility

Volatility is the standard deviation of returns over 10 years, changed from the 5 years first proposed. A product with fewer than 10 years of returns must use simulated past performance based on the history of the underlying assets, or on an appropriate benchmark where that is not possible.

### Step 2: Map volatility to a band

| Score | Standard deviation range |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Below 0.5% |
| 2 | 0.5% to below 2% |
| 3 | 2% to below 5% |
| 4 | 5% to below 9% |
| 5 | 9% to below 12% |
| 6 | 12% to below 16% |
| 7 | 16% to below 20% |
| 8 | 20% to below 30% |
| 9 | 30% to below 50% |
| 10 | 50% and above |

The FCA kept these bands despite pushback that a 12% volatility should score 4 or lower, and that S&P 500 products could land on a 6. Products that sit on the border of two buckets and drift between them during the year do not need a product summary update outside the yearly review.

### Step 3: Apply automatic scores

- **Score of 10:** products structured so that a consumer could lose more than their principal investment, with a warning to that effect.
- **Score of 9:** VCTs and EISs, because they invest in early-stage companies that are more likely to fail.
- **Highly leveraged and derivative products:** gearing and leverage amplify gains and losses, so these risks must be reflected. It is for the manufacturer to decide whether leverage reaches a high-risk threshold. Derivatives used to reduce risk are not automatically a 9.
- **Structured deposits with bank-account protection:** baseline score of 1, then adjust for credit or illiquidity risk.

### Step 4: Adjust the score

Upward adjustment is expected whenever the manufacturer sees a material risk the volatility misses. Examples are credit risk, which could leave a consumer unable to realise the investment, and concentration risk. The list of risks is non-exhaustive, and firms must adjust and disclose any other significant risk.

Downward adjustment is allowed only cautiously, and only in two cases: there is at least a 90% capital guarantee, or the initial score is not representative of overall risk, for example because the calculation window included extreme market anomalies.

### Step 5: Add the liquidity uplift

A CCI must add at least +1 to its score if either condition applies: it invests in illiquid assets, or it gives the retail investor a delay or added cost when trying to get money out. If both apply, +1 is added only once, to avoid double counting. No uplift is needed where the product is already a 9, or where illiquid assets are regularly traded on an exchange with transparent valuations.

Products with very low liquidity or that are not regularly priced must also carry a warning that this may affect the ability to redeem.

### Structured products

Structured product manufacturers must use the Value-at-Risk Equivalent Volatility (VEV) method to calculate volatility, then use the same bands. They must still adjust upward for other risks, particularly credit risk and the risk of difficulty getting money out. Structured capital-at-risk products no longer score an automatic 9 but need a label warning that they are complex and carry a greater risk of consumer misunderstanding. Capital guaranteed notes are scored from the volatility of the underlying assets, then adjusted for extra risks and the level of protection.

### The narrative

Manufacturers must also write a short risk and return description in plain language, balanced between risk and potential return. Distributors should supplement it along the consumer journey.

## 7. Past performance

Past performance returns as a chart, replacing the PRIIPs performance scenarios.

| Element | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Chart type | Line graph of the product's past 10 years of performance |
| Y-axis | Value in pounds of an initial investment of £10,000 |
| X-axis | Time, with a label that works for incomplete calendar years |
| Data points | At least monthly. Changed from the quarterly minimum first proposed, to reflect volatility |
| Short history | Products with between 3 months and 10 years of data show the data they have |
| Non-sterling products | The £10,000 is replaced by an equivalent amount divisible by 1,000 and of similar magnitude |
| Distributors | May substitute the investor's own initial investment amount |
| Mandatory statement | Prominent statement that the performance shown may not be representative of longer-term performance |
| Mergers | The earlier requirement to show each pre-merger fund separately was removed |

### The benchmark rules

The graph includes a benchmark chosen by the manufacturer. Where there is no appropriate benchmark, the manufacturer must explain how performance may be assessed. For authorised funds, the rules mirror COBS 4.5.12 to 4.5.14, so any target, constraining or comparator benchmark named in the prospectus must be included.

If a fund materially changed its objective and strategy, the period before the change must not be compared to a benchmark irrelevant to its previous strategy. The benchmark for that earlier period must be consistent with the fund's previous objectives.

Outside the product summary, the COBS 4 past performance rules are being aligned with these DISC rules, with consultation in early 2026 and finalisation before the end of the implementation period.

## 8. CCI versus PRIIPs at a glance

| Topic | EU PRIIPs KID | UK CCI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Document | Prescribed KID template | Product summary with no template or page limit |
| Costs headline | Summary cost indicator and Reduction in Yield | Ongoing Charge Figure in percent and pounds and pence |
| Transaction costs | Implicit and explicit | Explicit only, shown separately |
| Risk scale | 1 to 7 | 1 to 10, called risk and return |
| Risk window | 5 years | 10 years, simulated or benchmark data if shorter |
| Performance | Performance scenarios | Historical line graph against a benchmark, monthly points |
| Data to distributors | KID | Machine-readable file plus product summary |
